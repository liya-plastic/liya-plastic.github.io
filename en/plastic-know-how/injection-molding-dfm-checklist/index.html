Injection molding DFM: 7 checks before tooling

Uniform walls, ribs, bosses, draft, radii, gates, and ejection: a practical pre-tooling review that prevents expensive geometry problems from becoming mold problems.

A mold can reproduce geometry very consistently, but it cannot rescue geometry that fights polymer flow, cooling, shrinkage, or ejection. That is why the highest-leverage review happens before steel is cut.

Liya's internal DFM note reduces the review to three core rules: keep walls as uniform as the function allows, radius the corners that concentrate stress, and provide enough draft for the actual depth and texture. The seven checks below turn those rules into a repeatable design conversation.

Key takeaways

  • Prefer uniform nominal walls; core out thick masses instead of adding material for stiffness.
  • Keep rib and boss walls near 50–60% of the adjacent nominal wall as a starting point to reduce sink risk.
  • Draft depends on depth, resin, ejection, and texture; a textured surface usually needs materially more draft than a polished one.
  • Review gate, parting line, undercuts, and ejection together with cosmetics—not after the mold layout is fixed.

The seven checks

FeatureEngineering starting pointWhat goes wrong when ignored
1. Nominal wallKeep it as uniform as function allows; transition graduallyDifferential cooling, sink, voids, internal stress, and warp
2. RibsRoot thickness about 50–60% of the nominal wall; avoid excessive heightA thick rib prints through as a sink mark on the cosmetic face
3. BossesKeep boss walls near 0.6× the nominal wall and support them with ribsMass concentration, sink, cracking, and difficult ejection
4. DraftUse as much as the design permits; 0.5–1° may suit simple polished faces, while texture/depth need moreDrag, scuffing, ejector whitening, or a part that stays on the wrong mold half
5. Internal radiiUse a generous inside radius; 0.5× wall is a useful starting pointStress concentration, restricted flow, and local thickening
6. UndercutsEliminate, redesign, or intentionally plan side actions, lifters, or insertsTool complexity, maintenance, longer cycle, and higher risk
7. Gate and ejectionPlace them around fill, packing, appearance, and structural prioritiesWeld lines in critical areas, sink in thick zones, or ejection distortion

Why uniform wall thickness is the first question

Thick sections hold heat longer than thin sections. Their cores continue to contract after the outer skin has stiffened, so the surface may pull inward as a sink mark or the interior may form a void. Uneven cooling also creates uneven shrinkage and residual stress, which can appear later as warpage or cracking.

If a region needs more stiffness, first consider coring, ribs, gussets, curvature, or a different material grade. Simply making the wall thicker can add resin and cooling time while making dimensional behavior less predictable.

Draft is a system decision, not one universal angle

A polished, shallow wall may release with modest draft, while a deep wall, high-shrink resin, soft material, or textured face needs more. Texture behaves like thousands of microscopic undercuts. Liya's working guideline starts around 0.5–1° for simple smooth faces, then increases with wall depth and texture; deep or heavily textured surfaces must be reviewed with the actual mold maker and texture specification.

Apply draft early in the CAD model. Adding it after ribs, snaps, seals, and mating geometry are frozen often creates tolerance conflicts that are more expensive than designing the taper into the product architecture.

What a useful DFM handoff should show

  • A wall-thickness map with abrupt transitions highlighted.
  • Draft analysis by pull direction, including textured faces.
  • Proposed parting line, gate, ejector, side-action, and insert locations.
  • Cosmetic A-surfaces and areas where gate vestige, ejector marks, or weld lines are unacceptable.
  • Open decisions, ranked by product risk rather than by drawing convenience.
DFM is not a pass/fail report. It is a controlled trade-off between product function, appearance, tooling, cycle time, and repeatability.

FAQ

What rib thickness should an injection-molded part use?

A common engineering starting point is about 50–60% of the adjacent nominal wall. Resin, appearance, flow direction, and structural load can change the final value.

Is one degree of draft always enough?

No. Draft depends on wall depth, resin shrinkage, mold side, surface finish, texture depth, and ejection design. Textured or deep surfaces commonly require more.
