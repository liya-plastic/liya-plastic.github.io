Resin drying is more than heat: moisture, dew point, and hydrolysis

A hot hopper can still deliver wet resin. Learn why hygroscopic polymers need controlled dew point, residence time, airflow, and a sealed path to the machine.

Drying is not the same as heating. A heater can raise pellet temperature while humid air leaves bound moisture inside the resin. When hygroscopic engineering plastics such as PC, PA, PBT, PET, PMMA, and TPU enter a hot barrel with excess moisture, hydrolysis can cut molecular chains before the part even leaves the mold.

Silver streaks and bubbles are visible warnings, but the more serious result can be lost impact strength, brittleness, discoloration, electrical change, or delayed cracking. A stable drying system controls four things together: drying temperature, time, dry-air dew point, and airflow through the correct amount of material.

Key takeaways

  • For hygroscopic resins, temperature alone does not prove dryness; dew point and actual outlet moisture matter.
  • A common low-dew-point target for engineering resins is at or below −40°C, but the resin grade's TDS is the authority.
  • Hopper capacity must match hourly consumption × required residence time, or pellets leave before they are dry.
  • Dry resin can reabsorb moisture quickly, so closed conveying and sealed temporary storage are part of the drying process.

What hydrolysis changes

At molding temperatures, moisture can react with susceptible polymer chains and reduce molecular weight. The machine may still fill the cavity, yet the finished part can lose toughness and durability. Visual acceptance alone therefore cannot confirm material integrity.

A practical control plan distinguishes surface moisture from moisture diffused inside the pellet. Ambient hot-air dryers may work for non-hygroscopic materials or simple surface moisture, but strongly hygroscopic engineering resins generally require desiccant or another validated low-dew-point drying method.

Typical starting points—always verify the grade TDS

Resin familyTypical drying startWhy control is critical
PCAround 120°C for 3–4 h, low dew point; verify moisture limitHydrolysis can reduce toughness and optical quality
PA6 / PA66Around 80–90°C for 4–6 h with dry airRapid moisture uptake changes processing and final dimensions/properties
PBTAround 120°C for 3–4 h with low-dew-point airMoisture-driven chain scission can make parts brittle
PETRequires grade/process-specific crystallization and drying, often at higher temperatureVery moisture-sensitive; amorphous pellets can agglomerate if heated incorrectly
PMMA / TPUGrade-dependent controlled dryingMoisture can cause optical streaks, bubbles, tack, and property loss
These are knowledge-base reference ranges, not setup instructions. Reinforcement, flame retardant, color, recycled content, packaging history, and grade chemistry can change the correct condition.

Four drying failures that a temperature display will not catch

  • Dew point failure: the heater is on, but the desiccant wheel, regeneration, filter, or return-air loop is no longer producing dry air.
  • Residence-time failure: an oversized consumption rate or undersized hopper pulls pellets out before the required drying time.
  • Airflow failure: channeling, clogged filters, poor diffuser design, or an overfilled hopper prevents uniform dry-air contact.
  • Reabsorption: dried material is exposed in open bins, long unsealed lines, or an unsealed machine hopper before molding.

What to record for traceable drying

Record resin supplier, grade, lot, package-open time, dryer ID, set and actual temperature, inlet/return dew point, drying start time, hourly consumption, and moisture measurement when the part risk justifies it. Alarm history matters: a four-hour average can hide a short dew-point excursion that affected one lot.

When silver streaks appear, do not assume every streak is water. Air entrainment, volatile contamination, degradation, excessive shear, or poor venting can look similar. Confirm the drying evidence before changing the mold or forcing the process.

FAQ

Why is a hot-air dryer not always enough for engineering plastics?

It heats ambient air, so drying ability changes with humidity and may not remove moisture diffused inside hygroscopic pellets. A validated low-dew-point system creates a stronger moisture-driving force.

Does a dry-looking molded part prove the resin was dry?

No. Hydrolysis can reduce molecular weight and mechanical properties without an obvious surface defect. Moisture measurement and controlled drying records provide stronger evidence.
